[SCENE 01 — Boat at dawn]
The boat came in at dawn, the way Marisol always said it should. Iris Vaughn was twenty-two. She was the new relief keeper at Cape Vespers. Her aunt had died on this island the previous winter. Everyone said it was a fall. Iris did not believe it.

[SCENE 02 — The dock]
Halen Yoo was the supply-boat captain. She had known Marisol for ten years. She had known Iris since Iris was twelve. The first thing she said to Iris at the dock was: you don't have to do this. The second thing she said was: I will come every Thursday. Iris heard the third thing Halen did not say: I am afraid for you.

[SCENE 03 — The tower]
The tower stair was a hundred and twelve steps. Iris had counted them at fourteen. She counted them again at twenty-two, the way a person touches a bruise to see if it still hurts. At the top, her aunt's woolen coat was still on the hook by the door. Iris put it on. It fit.

[SCENE 04 — The cottage]
The cottage had been half-cleared by the service. Marisol's books were stacked against the wall by someone who did not know which books mattered. Iris set her bag on the table and put her hand on the back of her aunt's chair. She would re-cane it before she left.

[SCENE 05 — First night]
Iris woke at three in the morning with the fog pressing at the window. She got up. She heard a sound — the low rumble of a small marine engine, very close. No boat arrived at the dock. She told herself it was the wind in the eaves, the way she had been telling herself things since her mother died, and after a while she believed it. She did not sleep again.

[SCENE 06 — Daily routine]
The work was small and the days were long. Iris cleaned the lens. She wrote the keeper's log entry her aunt had never finished. She walked the path twice a day. She did not yet write down the engine in the fog. She did not yet understand that she was the kind of person who could not write down a thing that frightened her.

[SCENE 07 — The box under the floor]
On the fifth day, the kitchen floor buckled. There had been a slow leak under the stove for years; her aunt had meant to fix it and not. Iris knelt with a pry bar and lifted the board. Under it, in a tight, dry, surprisingly clean space, was a small water-tight metal box. It had held her aunt's passport when Iris was a child. Iris took it to the lamp room and set it under the lens.

[SCENE 08 — The first decoded entry]
The notebook was in code. Marisol had taught Iris the code at twelve, on a rainy afternoon in this kitchen. 'If you ever need to write something you don't want anyone to read,' Marisol had said, 'you write it in this. It's just letters and numbers but it's ours.' Iris had thought it was a game. She decoded the first page. Someone comes by on Thursdays who is not on the service list. Iris closed the notebook. She opened it. She closed it again.

[SCENE 09 — Halen returns (Thursday 1)]
On Thursday the supply boat came. Halen brought the mail, two cartons of eggs, a new headlamp, a sack of red onions. In the kitchen, while Halen put the eggs away, Iris said: 'Halen, did my aunt ever mention a man named Renner to you?' Halen's hands stopped moving for the length of one breath. 'Your aunt saw things on this island that weren't there,' Halen said. 'Don't chase it.'

[SCENE 10 — The path at night]
Iris walked down the path with a flashlight. The boathouse door was padlocked with the service padlock. The knot on the hasp was a fisherman's loop, not a knot the service used. She photographed it. She photographed it again from a different angle.

[SCENE 11 — Second journal entry]
Iris decoded the next entry. Her aunt photographed the Visitor. The photograph was missing from the journal — but a small negative strip was hidden behind the journal's spine. The fourth frame showed a figure at the end of the dock in the early dawn light, bending over a crate. The face was in profile, half-shadowed. Iris did not recognize the face. She did not need to recognize it. Her aunt had named it in the notebook. The face was a man named Renner.

[SCENE 12 — The contact sheet]
Iris developed the negative in her aunt's old enlarger. She made a contact sheet and a single print. She put the print in the green notebook and put the green notebook back in the metal box and put the metal box back under the floor and put the board back.

[SCENE 13 — The inspector arrives]
Inspector Delacroix came on the next Thursday. She was warm, professional. She had a tidy way of standing that Iris recognized from her aunt: the way a person stands who has spent twenty years on government boats in heavy weather. 'I was the one who found her,' Wren said. Iris let that sit. Wren waited, professionally, for the silence to break. Iris did not break it.

[SCENE 14 — Midpoint reveal: 'the lens is the only honest witness']
At the bottom of the tower, Wren said, conversationally, 'Your aunt always said the lens was the only honest witness on this island.' Iris did not move. The line was from the green notebook. Iris had not told anyone she had the notebook. She smiled. She said, 'She used to say a lot of things.' Wren smiled back. Wren left on the tender. Iris sat in the lamp room with the lens turning above her and the contact sheet in her hand and understood, in a single slow breath, that her aunt had not fallen on the path. Her aunt had been killed by a man who came to the island on Thursdays, and the inspector knew.

[SCENE 15 — Mist closing]
Iris sat with her hands on the contact sheet and waited for her breath to come back, and after a while it came back. Then she went downstairs and packed the contact sheet, the print, and the negatives into the metal box and the metal box into her bag and her bag into the bottom of the well at the cottage, where the water was cold and the box would not be found. She did not yet know what she would do with what she had. She only knew that she had it.

[SCENE 16 — The third journal entry — Renner named]
Iris went back to the green notebook. The next entry named Renner: Otis Renner, 53, retired fisherman out of Neah Bay, who had used the boathouse as a drop point for forty years and paid the inspector since 1995. Her aunt had written: 'If I die on this island, it was Renner.' Iris read the dates and the amounts. She read the names. She read the names of the three keepers before her who had died on this island, and she read that none of them had died the way the service said they had died.

[SCENE 17 — The boathouse]
The service padlock was locked, but the boathouse door was wood and the frame was rotten where the salt had worked into it for forty years. Iris pried the frame with the back of an axe. Inside, under the tarp, was a wooden crate with brass corners — cartons of cigarettes, three small canvas sacks, a length of fishing line. She took one carton and one sack. She photographed everything. She shut the boathouse and pried the frame back into place.

[SCENE 18 — Halen returns (Thursday 3) — confession]
Halen came up on the Petrel alone. She came into the cottage without speaking. She shut the door. She stood with her back to it. 'I knew,' Halen said. 'I knew your aunt suspected. I told her not to chase it. She didn't listen to me. You listen to me. Pack your bag. We are leaving on this tide.' Iris did not pack her bag. She set the contact sheet on the table, the negative strip, the green notebook, the carton, the canvas sack. Halen said, 'I will come for you Thursday morning. We leave together. Whatever you have, we take it to the Coast Guard in Port Angeles.'

[SCENE 19 — The storm warning — the bookmarked page]
The shortwave carried a small-craft advisory: gale warning, eight-to-ten-foot swell, the Petrel would not be able to dock. Iris climbed to the lamp room. She took the old crank handle from the closet. She read the page in the keeper's manual her aunt had bookmarked with a gull's feather. She read it twice. She sat under the lens with the wind in the glass and the lamp turning above her and her aunt's coat around her shoulders and she understood, in a slow breath, what her aunt had bookmarked for her.

[SCENE 20 — The visitor]
At half past two a boat engine sounded through the rain. It was not the Petrel. The Petrel had a diesel; this was a gasoline engine, older, with a knock. Iris went down to the cottage, took the metal box from the well, put the print, the negatives, the contact sheet, the notebook into a waterproof bag and put the bag inside her aunt's coat. She went up to the lamp room. She took the old crank handle from the closet. A man came up the dock. He knocked on the tower door with the pipe. 'I know you're up there,' he said. 'I knew your aunt too.'

[SCENE 21 — The fog signal]
Iris climbed to the catwalk. She set the crank handle into the lens cam socket and turned. The lens, which had been turning on the photocell, stopped. She turned the crank again. The lens began to turn by hand. She turned it until the lens pointed at the fog-signal cam. She set the crank handle into the cam socket. She turned. The cam engaged the fog-signal whistle. The whistle had not sounded in thirty years. It sounded now — long, low, iron — long-short-long-short-long. The old Coast Guard SOS for lighthouse keepers before radio, the one her aunt had taught her at twelve, on the same rainy afternoon as the code. Out on the water, beyond the storm, a small diesel engine changed note. The Petrel came through the channel at the top of the engine's range.

[SCENE 22 — After]
Dawn. The Coast Guard came. They took Renner. Iris gave the waterproof bag to the officer. Wren Delacroix was arrested on the mainland at eight that morning; Iris heard it on the shortwave. Renner sat on a dock cleat with the pipe in his hand. Iris said: 'You killed my aunt.' He said: 'I did.' She said: 'Why?' He said: 'The route fed my family for forty years. Your aunt should have stayed in the kitchen.' Iris did not hit him. She did not speak to him again. She sat on a dock cleat and waited. Six months later a postcard arrived at Bose's mainland home. Iris had enrolled in a maritime-archaeology program. The postcard was a photograph of Cape Vespers from the water. On the back, in the code Marisol had taught her, Iris had written: 'For Marisol.' The lens on Cape Vespers kept turning. It does not lie.
