# Reference Analysis 02 — *The Scarlet Plague* (London, 1915)

**Category:** Reference B — Apocalyptic survival | **Words:** ~20,298 | **Status:** PASS

## A. Narrative architecture
Frame narrative + retrospective survivor account. An old man (Granser) and grandson Edwin walk an overgrown railway embankment 60 years post-plague. Edwin asks Granser to tell the story. Flashback: plague → panic → mass death → grid failure → food collapse → roaming gangs → regression to barbarism → forgetting how to read → forgetting plague's name. Midpoint: Granser realizes death of educated is death of species' memory; second half about loss of culture, not life. Open ending: cycle restarting, old man dying, boys won't learn.

**Lesson:** Apocalypse gains power told *after* by a survivor narrating into regression. Reader sees both apocalypse and its long sad second act — the forgetting.

## B. Survival mechanics
Salt-water-cured meat, dried salmon, acorns, roots, goat meat. Caves, huts, brush shelters. Mild coastal California removes winter as threat — threat is purely social. Plague symptoms scarlet, rapid, fatal. Navigation dangerous (wild dogs, "gringos"). Tools: pocket knife, fire-stick, club, canoe. Loss of gunpowder, matches, written records mapped decade by decade. Biggest scarcity is *knowledge* — books rot, paper burns, no one can read.

## C. Character design
Granser: largely passive in present frame, but flashback shows decisive choices (fleeing city, keeping revolver, hiding, seeking survivors). Most important act: *trying to teach*. Strengths: literate, patient, observant. Weaknesses: physically weak, mournful, at mercy of grandsons. Wound: cultural amnesia — can't recall what plague killed family.

## D. Suspense
Withheld information (reader doesn't know what "chauffeur" meant; neither do boys). Knowledge erasure dramatized as linguistic drift. False assumptions (each hope of civilization remnant removed). Mystery is not "who did it" but "how did we forget." Betrayal (friendly survivor kills Smith for revolver). Reversals (boys' laughter at alphabet — kindness to mockery).

## E. Scene craft
Each scene teaches a single forgotten thing (railroad, alphabet, money, plague). Conflict: generational — boys have no frame for "money," "railroad," "germs." Grief at memory; pride in survival; despair at forgetting. Boy's questions as exposition prompts (modern documentary-narration technique). Slow, elegiac pacing.

## F. Adaptation potential
Overgrown railway embankment; coastal cliff camp; ruined Berkeley campus; plague-choked city street; foggy beach. World reclaimed by redwood and scrub oak; string of glass beads as currency. Old man tracing letters in wet sand. Flashback contains plague's first week — collapsing crowds, body cart, man shot for can of peaches.

**Lesson for our film:** Long second act of an apocalypse is forgetting. YA pilot can dramatize this in miniature by having protagonist forget one specific thing (a name, recipe, tune) and feel cultural loss as personal wound.
