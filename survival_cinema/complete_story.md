## Part One — Arrival

The boat came in at dawn, the way Marisol always said it should.

Iris Vaughn stood on the bow of the *Petrel* with a camera in her hand and a coat she had not yet earned around her shoulders. The coat was her aunt's; it still smelled of her aunt; Iris did not know yet whether wearing it was a way to remember or a way to refuse to let go.

Cape Vespers rose out of the grey water like the spine of something drowned. White tower, black band, the lamp turning at the top in a slow circle that had been turning since before Iris was born and would, the service said, keep turning long after she was gone. She had been here four summers. She had not been here since her aunt died.

"You don't have to do this," Halen Yoo said, behind her. The supply-boat captain was a compact woman with salt-bleached short hair and a way of speaking that never invited an answer. "You can turn around right now and nobody on the mainland will think less of you."

"I will think less of me," Iris said.

"That's not a reason to be here."

"That's the only reason anyone is anywhere," Iris said, and stepped off the bow onto the dock with her bag over her shoulder and her camera around her neck.

The dock was the same. The path was the same. The cottage, when she let herself in, was the same and not the same — half her aunt's books had been taken by the service, the rest stacked against the wall by someone who did not know which books mattered. Iris set her bag on the table and stood in the kitchen with her hand on the back of a chair and let herself breathe.

The chair was Marisol's chair. Iris had sat in this chair every summer of her childhood and read *Two Years Before the Mast* under the table while her aunt braided onion tops above the stove. The chair had not been re-caned since the funeral. Iris ran her thumb across the broken strand and knew she would re-cane it before she left.

The shortwave crackled on the shelf. *Vespers, Vespers, this is Bose on the mainland, you got anybody up there yet?*

Iris crossed the room and took the mic. "Bose, this is Iris Vaughn. I'm up here."

The pause was long enough to mean something. *You Marisol's girl,* Bose said.

"I'm Marisol's girl."

*Welcome back to Vespers, kid. You need anything, you call me. I'll be here.*

"Thank you, Bose." Iris set the mic down. She took a breath. She climbed the tower.

The tower stair was a hundred and twelve steps. Iris had counted them at fourteen. She counted them again at twenty-two, the way a person touches a bruise to see if it still hurts. The lamp room at the top was cold. The lens — a first-order Fresnel, the keeper's manual said, although the service had stopped using that term years ago — sat under its glass dome like the inside of a watch. The LED lamp turned on its photocell. Iris found her aunt's woolen coat hanging on the hook by the door, where someone had left it for her, or had not had the heart to take it down.

She put it on. It fit.

That night she dreamed of being locked in a closet at five years old and woke at three in the morning with her heart in her mouth and the fog pressing at the window. She got up. She stood at the kitchen window with the lamp on and the shortwave off. She heard a sound — the low rumble of a small marine engine, very close.

No boat arrived at the dock.

She told herself it was the wind in the eaves, the way she had been telling herself things since her mother died, and after a while she believed it. She went back to bed. She did not sleep again.

## Part Two — Settling and Wrongness

The work was small and the days were long. Iris cleaned the lamp. She replaced the keeper's log entry her aunt had never finished. She walked the path twice a day, morning and evening, the way her aunt had taught her: look at the dock, look at the boathouse, look at the path, look at the lamp. Anything out of place, you write it down.

She did not write down the engine in the fog. She did not yet understand that she was the kind of person who could not write down a thing that frightened her.

On the fifth day, the kitchen floor buckled. There had been a slow leak under the stove for years; her aunt had meant to fix it and not. Iris knelt with a pry bar and lifted the board. Under it, in a tight, dry, surprisingly clean space, was a small water-tight metal box.

Iris knew the box. It had held her aunt's passport when Iris was a child.

She took it to the lamp room and set it under the lens and opened it. Inside was a notebook with a green cover, a pencil, and a strip of negatives in a paper sleeve.

The notebook was in code. Marisol had taught Iris the code at twelve, on a rainy afternoon in this kitchen, with the wood stove ticking. *If you ever need to write something you don't want anyone to read,* Marisol had said, *you write it in this. It's just letters and numbers but it's ours.*

Iris had thought it was a game.

She sat on the floor under the lens and decoded the first page.

*Someone comes by on Thursdays who is not on the service list. I have not seen his face yet. The lens shows me only fog. I do not know what he is taking from the boathouse but he is taking something.*

Iris closed the notebook. She opened it. She closed it again.

She had been told her aunt died of a fall on the path between the boathouse and the cottage. The county coroner had said: accidental, no sign of foul play. Iris had not asked any questions because she had not had any. She had been in Portland with a boy she did not love, doing a job she did not love, when her aunt died.

She read the second entry.

*I rigged a trip-line at the boathouse last Thursday. It was cut at dawn. I am going to start keeping a camera in the lamp room with the long lens. If he comes when I think he is going to come, I will photograph him from the catwalk at four in the morning.*

Iris read the third entry.

*He came. I have his picture. I have hidden the picture where the service will not find it but I will know where to look. His name is Renner. He is a fisherman, retired. He has been using this boathouse since before I was the keeper. The inspector knows.*

Iris set the notebook down. She picked the notebook up. She read the third entry a third time. The third entry did not change.

She went to the shelf and took down the keeper's manual. The pages fell open to the page her aunt had bookmarked with a feather from a gull: *Cape Vespers — Automated 1984. The fog signal was decommissioned in 1994. The clockwork of the signal remains intact in the lamp room; the lens rotation may be hand-engaged via the old crank handle in the event of a power failure; the signal cam may be hand-rotated for ceremonial use or training.*

Iris did not know yet what she was reading for. She knew only that her aunt had bookmarked this page in the last summer of her life.

She went back down to the kitchen and put the kettle on. While the water heated, she lifted the board again, and looked at the empty space under the floor, and put the board back, and stood in the kitchen, and drank her tea.

On Thursday the supply boat came. Halen brought the mail, two cartons of eggs, a new headlamp, a sack of red onions. Iris helped carry them up to the cottage. In the kitchen, while Halen put the eggs away, Iris said: "Halen, did my aunt ever mention a man named Renner to you?"

Halen's hands stopped moving for the length of one breath. "Where did you hear that name?"

"In the keeper's log. She wrote it down."

"That's not in the keeper's log, Iris."

Iris did not say anything. Halen turned and looked at her with a look Iris would remember later: a look of someone who has just realized a worse thing than the one she came to prevent.

"Your aunt saw things on this island that weren't there," Halen said. "Don't chase it."

"Things that weren't there," Iris said.

"Things she couldn't prove. Things that weren't worth chasing. You're twenty-two years old. You don't have to be here. Go home. Take photographs in Portland. Marisol would not have wanted you here."

"Marisol is why I am here."

Halen left. Iris stood on the dock until the *Petrel* was a smudge against the mainland and walked back up the path. At the boathouse she stopped. The door was padlocked. The padlock was the service padlock. The knot on the hasp was a fisherman's loop, not a knot the service used.

She photographed it. She photographed it again from a different angle.

The next night she walked down with a flashlight and stood in the path and listened. She heard gulls and surf and the distant automated foghorn of the Biddle light, ten miles south. She heard the soft tick of the lamp's gear rotation at the top of the tower, audible at the base of the tower in the right wind. She did not hear an engine.

She went back up to the cottage and lit the lamp and decoded her aunt's next entry.

*Renner came on Thursday at four in the morning. He took three sacks from the boathouse. He did not see me. The sacks were heavy for their size. I think they are not fish. I think they are not mail. I think they are not the service's.*

Iris set the notebook down. She thought about the strip of negatives in the box. She had not yet looked at the negatives.

She took the negatives to the lamp room and held them up to the lens. The negatives were a strip of four frames. Three were dark. One — the fourth — showed a figure at the end of the dock in the early dawn light, bending over a crate. The face was in profile, half-shadowed. Iris did not recognize the face. She did not need to recognize it. Her aunt had named it in the notebook. The face was a man named Renner.

Iris developed the negative in the cottage's old enlarger, which her aunt had kept for photographing the lamp. She made a contact sheet and a single print. She put the print in the green notebook between two pages and put the green notebook back in the metal box and put the metal box back under the floor and put the board back and went up to the lamp room and stood under the lens.

The lens turned. The fog came in. Iris sat under the lens and read the green notebook all the way through in one night. By morning she had read it twice.

The notebook was not the journal of a woman who fell on a path. The notebook was the journal of a woman who had spent the last summer of her life trying to prove that someone had been using her island for forty years, and who had finally gotten a photograph, and who had told exactly one person: the inspector.

The inspector's name was Wren Delacroix.

## Part Three — The Inspector

Inspector Delacroix came on the next Thursday.

Iris heard the *Petrel's* engine and a second engine behind it — a small Coast Guard tender, painted white, that Iris had not seen before. From the dock came Wren Delacroix, age forty, with a clipboard and a smile and a handshake. She had a tidy way of standing that Iris recognized from her aunt: the way a person stands who has spent twenty years on government boats in heavy weather.

"You must be Iris," Wren said. "Your aunt spoke about you."

"You knew my aunt."

"I inspected this light for twelve years. I was the one who found her."

Iris let that sit. Wren waited, professionally, for the silence to break. Iris did not break it. After a moment Wren said, "May I see the lamp?"

Iris led her up. At the top, Wren ran her hand along the lens the way a librarian runs a hand along a shelf — not possessively, just to be sure. She checked the lamp's battery log. She checked the photocell. She asked Iris about the well water, about the cottage roof, about the shortwave. Iris answered. Wren listened. Wren did not ask about the journals.

At the bottom of the tower, Wren said, conversationally, "Your aunt always said the lens was the only honest witness on this island."

Iris did not move.

The line was from the green notebook. Iris had read it the night before: *The lens is the only honest witness on this island. It does not lie about the weather, the sea, or the men who come to my dock.*

Iris had not told anyone she had the notebook.

She smiled. She said, "She used to say a lot of things."

Wren smiled back. She left on the tender. Iris sat in the lamp room with the lens turning above her and the contact sheet in her hand and understood, in a single slow breath, that her aunt had not fallen on the path. Her aunt had been killed by a man who came to the island on Thursdays, and the inspector knew.

She did not cry. She did not stand. She sat with her hands on the contact sheet and waited for her breath to come back and after a while it came back. Then she went downstairs and packed the contact sheet, the print, and the negatives into the metal box and the metal box into her bag and her bag into the bottom of the well at the cottage, where the water was cold and the box would not be found.

She did not yet know what she would do with what she had. She only knew that she had it.

## Part Four — Becoming Proactive

She went back to the green notebook. The next entry was three weeks before her aunt's death.

*Renner's name is Otis Renner, 53, retired fisherman out of Neah Bay. He ran black-market cigarettes up to Vancouver Island for thirty years. He used the boathouse as his drop because the service stopped staffing the light in 1984. He has paid the inspector since 1995. He paid the inspector before me, too. I have written down the dates and the amounts. I have written down the boat. I have written down the names of the three keepers before me who died on this island and I have written down that none of them died the way the service said they died.*

Iris read the dates and the amounts. She read the names. She read the name of the inspector before Wren, who had retired in 2002. She read the name of the keeper who had died in 1995 — a heart attack on the tower stair, the service said. She read the name of the keeper who had died in 2007 — drowned from the dock, the service said. She read the name of the keeper who had died in 2014 — a fall on the path, the service said.

The keeper who had died in 2014 was Marisol's predecessor. Marisol had been the relief keeper who took the job after that fall.

Iris put the notebook down. She went out to the boathouse.

The service padlock was locked, but the boathouse door was wood and the frame was rotten where the salt had worked into it for forty years. Iris pried the frame with the back of an axe. The door swung open.

Inside the boathouse was the old aluminum skiff with its broken fuel line, a coil of rope, a fuel can, a tarp. Under the tarp was a wooden crate, hand-made, with brass corners. Iris lifted the tarp.

The crate held cartons of cigarettes, three small canvas sacks, and a length of fishing line. Iris took one carton of cigarettes and one canvas sack. She photographed the crate, the cartons, the sacks, the fishing line, the inside of the boathouse from four angles.

She shut the boathouse and pried the frame back into place. The padlock still locked. The padlock had always locked. She went up to the cottage and called Bose.

Bose answered on the second ring. Iris said, "Bose, my aunt's death was not an accident."

The pause was the longest of Iris's life. *I know,* Bose said.

"You knew?"

"I been telling the service for ten years, kid. Three keepers. None of them died the way the service said. Your aunt was the fourth. I told her the same thing I'm telling you — trust the lamp, not the inspector. The lamp doesn't lie."

"Bose, the inspector knows."

"I know she knows."

"Bose, what do I do?"

The pause was shorter this time. *You come home,* Bose said. *You pack your aunt's things and you come home on Thursday and you give what you have to the Coast Guard, not the service. The service is in it.*

"Bose, the service *is* the inspector."

"I know, kid. That's why I'm telling you to give it to the Coast Guard."

Iris set the mic down. She sat at the kitchen table with her hand on the back of the chair and let herself breathe. The wind picked up. The lamp began to turn a little faster, the way it did in a coming storm.

Thursday came.

Halen came up on the *Petrel* alone. She came into the cottage without speaking. She shut the door. She stood with her back to it.

"I knew," Halen said. "I knew your aunt suspected. I told her not to chase it. She didn't listen to me. You listen to me. Pack your bag. We are leaving on this tide."

"Halen, the service is in it."

"I know."

"The inspector is in it."

"I know."

"Halen — how long have you known?"

Halen's face did a thing Iris had never seen. "Since the third keeper. Since 2007. Since the man who drowned from the dock. He didn't drown, Iris. He was a friend of mine."

"Then why didn't you stop it?"

Halen's face did another thing. "Because I am a coward," she said, "and because I am not done being a coward. Pack your bag."

Iris did not pack her bag. She set the contact sheet on the table and the negative strip beside it and the green notebook beside that. She set the carton of cigarettes and the canvas sack on the table. She said, "I am not leaving without this."

Halen looked at the table. Halen looked at Iris. Halen said, "I will come for you Thursday morning. We leave together. Whatever you have, we take it to the Coast Guard in Port Angeles. Not the service. Not anyone else."

Iris said, "Thursday morning."

Halen said, "Thursday morning. Set the lamp to burning at three. I will be at the dock by four. Don't let anyone on this island between now and then."

Iris said, "What if the weather turns?"

Halen looked at her. "Then we wait one more week."

The shortwave crackled. The small-craft advisory came in for Thursday morning: gale warning, eight-to-ten-foot swell, the *Petrel* would not be able to dock.

Iris set the mic down. Halen set her hat on the table. Halen said, "Then we wait one more week."

Halen left on the tide. Iris stood on the dock and watched her go. The wind was already up. The lamp at the top of the tower turned, faster now, the way it did in a coming storm.

Iris went up to the lamp room. She took the old crank handle from the closet. She read the page in the keeper's manual her aunt had bookmarked with a gull's feather. She read it twice.

She sat under the lens with the wind in the glass and the lamp turning above her and her aunt's coat around her shoulders and she understood, in a slow breath, what her aunt had bookmarked for her.

## Part Five — Thursday

The storm broke at two in the morning.

Iris was already up. She had not slept. She had sat in the lamp room in her aunt's coat and read the green notebook a fourth time and a fifth. At one she had heard the buoy at the entrance to the channel begin to ring. At two the rain came in a sheet.

At half past two a boat engine sounded through the rain.

It was not the *Petrel*. The *Petrel* had a diesel; this was a gasoline engine, older, with a knock.

Iris went down to the cottage. She took the metal box from the well. She took the print, the negatives, the contact sheet, the notebook. She put them in a waterproof bag and put the bag inside her aunt's coat. She went up to the lamp room. She took the old crank handle from the closet.

The boat engine came closer. A flashlight played along the dock.

Iris went up to the catwalk around the lamp room. She did not turn on the lamp. She watched the dock.

A man came up the dock. He was tall, gray-haired, broad in the shoulder, with a fisherman's rolling walk. He had a flashlight in one hand and a length of pipe in the other. He came up the path to the cottage. He went into the cottage. He came out of the cottage. He stood in the path with the flashlight pointed at the tower door.

Iris did not move.

The man crossed to the tower door. He tried the door. The door was locked. Iris had locked it from the inside. He knocked once, politely, with the pipe.

Iris did not answer.

The man said, through the door, "I know you're up there. I knew your aunt too. I am not here to hurt you. I am here to take what is yours to take and go. I am too old to hurt anyone. Open the door."

Iris did not open the door. She had the crank handle in her hand. She had the keeper's manual open on the floor of the lamp room at the page her aunt had bookmarked. She set the crank handle into the socket on the lens housing and turned. The lens, which had been turning on the photocell, stopped. She turned the crank again. The lens began to turn by hand. She turned it until the lens pointed at the fog-signal cam. She set the crank handle into the cam socket. She turned.

The cam engaged the fog-signal whistle.

The fog-signal whistle had not sounded in thirty years. It sounded now — a long, low, iron note, then a short, then a long, then a short, then a long. Long-short-long-short-long. The old Coast Guard SOS for lighthouse keepers before radio, the one her aunt had taught her at twelve, on the same rainy afternoon as the code.

Iris turned the cam. The whistle sounded. The wind took the sound out across the water.

The man at the tower door stopped knocking.

Iris turned the cam. The whistle sounded again. Long-short-long-short-long.

The man ran down the path to the dock.

Iris turned the cam. The whistle sounded. Long-short-long-short-long.

Out on the water, beyond the storm, a small diesel engine changed note. The *Petrel*, which had not been able to dock and had been lying off the channel mouth, had heard the whistle. She came through the channel at the top of the engine's range.

The man reached his boat. He cast off the line. His gasoline engine would not start. Iris could hear him cranking it through the wind. Halen's diesel came through the channel. The two boats met at the dock. Halen had the Coast Guard on the shortwave. The Coast Guard cutter was already out of Port Angeles, summoned by Bose.

The man did not run. He sat down on the dock with the pipe in his hand and waited. Halen put a line on his boat. Iris came down from the tower in her aunt's coat with the crank handle in one hand and the waterproof bag in the other. She walked down the path to the dock. She stood in front of the man. The rain fell on both of them.

"You killed my aunt," Iris said.

The man did not look up. "I did," he said.

"You killed three keepers before her."

The man did not look up. "I did," he said.

"Why?"

The man said, "The route fed my family for forty years. The service took the lighthouse and broke the deal. I was restoring the deal. Your aunt should have stayed in the kitchen."

Iris did not hit him. She did not speak to him again. She sat on a dock cleat and waited for the Coast Guard.

## Part Six — After

The cutter came at dawn. They took Renner. Halen took the *Petrel* in alongside. Iris gave the waterproof bag to the Coast Guard officer. The officer read the notebook. The officer looked at the contact sheet. The officer looked at Iris.

The officer said, "Ma'am, this is enough. We will need a statement."

Iris said, "I will give you one in Port Angeles."

Wren Delacroix was arrested on the mainland at eight that morning. Iris heard it on the shortwave on the way back. She did not celebrate. She sat in the bow of the *Petrel* with her aunt's coat around her shoulders and her camera in her lap and watched Cape Vespers recede.

Halen came and sat beside her. Halen said, "What will you do now?"

Iris said, "I won't be back, Halen."

"I know."

"But I'll write."

Six months later a postcard arrived at Bose's mainland home. Iris had enrolled in a maritime-archaeology program in California. The postcard was a photograph of Cape Vespers from the water, taken from a research vessel in afternoon light. On the back, in the code Marisol had taught her, Iris had written: *For Marisol. — Iris.*

Bose, who had never learned the code, did not need to read it. He tacked the postcard to the wall above his shortwave, where it stayed.

The lens on Cape Vespers kept turning. It does not lie.
